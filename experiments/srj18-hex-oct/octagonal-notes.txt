Octagonal grid interpretation and experimental controls
=====================================================

The octagonal variant is an eight-neighbor square lattice: 0, 45, 90, 135,
180, 225, 270 and 315 degree headings. Regular octagons cannot tessellate
alone. A literal octagon/square tiling would be a different graph and is
not the graph implemented here.

Grid pitch is the cardinal neighbor distance in millimeters. Diagonal
steps cost sqrt(2) times pitch. The admissible octile heuristic exactly
matches unobstructed graph distance; it does not make diagonal wires
artificially cheap. The provider exposes that distance, while the shared
A* engine uses the Euclidean distance to the goal-candidate bounding box
for both graph variants: a consistent, admissible lower bound when there
are several possible breakout/tree targets. The source and target physical
pad positions must
connect through independently checked breakouts to the nearest feasible
grid vertices. Boundary bounds and copper clearance belong to the shared
routing engine and do not clamp nearest-node calculations.

Equal-pitch hexagonal and octagonal graphs have different point density.
For a triangular lattice with six neighbors and neighbor spacing h, area
per vertex is sqrt(3)/2 * h^2. Square lattice area per vertex is o^2.
To compare equal point density, use o=h*sqrt(sqrt(3)/2), about 0.930605*h.
At equal neighbor spacing, hex has about 15.47 percent more vertices per
unit area. Report which control was used; grid geometry can otherwise be
confounded with resolution and runtime.

There is a geometric reason to test eight directions. In an unbounded,
obstacle-free direction graph with k uniformly spaced headings, worst-case
path length divided by Euclidean length is sec(pi/k). That gives 1.154701
for six hexagonal directions and 1.082392 for eight octagonal directions.
For example the octagonal worst case is a displacement 22.5 degrees from
a cardinal heading. This is a directional quantization bound, not a claim
of achieved improvement on SRJ18: pads, obstacles, vias, breakout lengths,
rip-up policies and node spacing can dominate actual board results.

Dataset interpretation matters. Across the sixteen inputs, 702 of 2,896
connection records have one terminal and require no new route. A complete
singleton net is not evidence of routing success. Multi-terminal nets
should contribute terminalCount-1 required spanning links; report links
completed as well as net completion. The dataset uses two, four and six
layers depending on the board, and rect/oval obstacle records with optional
ccwRotationDegrees. Rotation must be applied to obstacle clearance checks;
oval records describe capsule-shaped pads rather than elliptical pads.

Meaningful provider checks in src/octGrid.test.ts verify nearest-node
choice against surrounding physical vertices and octile costs against
Dijkstra paths using physical edge lengths, including negative indices.
Six additional independent checks in src/octRouterGeometry.test.ts verify
diagonal obstacle cuts, a legal diagonal despite blocked adjacent cardinal
vertices, finite trace width/clearance, through vias blocked by an inner
layer, rotated rectangles/capsule ovals, and exact endpoint coordinates
preserved through breakouts. All ten checks passed before suite execution.

Shared algorithm limitations: local breakouts reach about 1.6 mm and have
a candidate cap; the closest terminal pair is attempted first; the next
branch targets at most the closest 32 existing tree vertices. A failed
initial pair or branch ends that connection without trying a different
pair/branch ordering. There is no rip-up. These choices are identical for
the hexagonal and octagonal variants and can dominate graph geometry.
Measured SRJ18 results are recorded separately by the suite orchestrator.
